## 2. What actually determines the score (verified in the repo, not assumed)

- `score += dt` per tick with `dt = 1/10`, plus small terms (fruit eaten adds `energy/1000`; losing an
  agent costs `energy/100`). So **score ≈ ticks / 10**: 1,800 means 18,000 ticks alive.
- The simulator runs to at most **30,000 ticks** (3,000 sim-seconds) — that is the 3,000-point ceiling.
- `README.md:186` (and the observed behaviour): the run ends when **accumulated response wait reaches
  600 s**, so in practice `score ≈ min(ticks, 600 s ÷ per-tick latency)`.
  - At our measured 9-20 ms/tick, 18,000 ticks needs 33 ms/tick — we have roughly **2x latency margin**.
  - Early scores of ~580 were not "bad policy", they were the ~5,400-tick latency ceiling of an era
    when we answered in ~110 ms/request. That is fixed now.
- **Conclusion: 1,800 is not blocked by latency or by energy. It is blocked by the policy.**

### Reachability (measured, `experiments/w_reach.py`)
- World energy yield over 18,000 ticks: **55,405** (seed 1130) and **57,072** (seed 1131).
- Cost to keep **one** lineage alive for 18,000 ticks: **~3,800 energy = 7%** of what the world yields.
- Our fleets die at tick 4,624 / 4,896 **with 6,469 / 6,505 fruit energy still standing on the map.**
- **The world is access-limited, not energy-limited, by a margin of ~15x.**

### Why they die (measured, `experiments/w_lockout_diag.py`, 3 seeds)
| cause | share of deaths |
|---|---|
| starved | 32-51% |
| eaten by predators | 23-41% |
| old age | 16-27% |

- **92-96% of predation deaths happen below the sprint-lockout threshold** (`environment.py:512`:
  below 100 energy the sim clamps movement to walking speed, so an exhausted agent cannot outrun a
  predator that sprints 15 to our walk 10).
- **Blind 76-87% of ticks**: 0.26-0.42 fruits visible per agent on average.
- Facing changes the geometry measurably: fleeing while *facing* the predator opens the gap by
  **+1.583 / +1.359 / +0.477** units/tick, while turned away the gap *closes* (−0.126 / +0.091 /
  **+4.221** on the third seed).
- Predators are never removed and spawn roughly as **t²** (≈16 by 18,000 ticks), so the mid-game is
  where a small fleet gets overwhelmed.

## 4. Proven (evidence-backed, keep these)

1. **Policy transfer is real**: the same controller scores the same in simulation and on the official
   grader (0 errors across every validation).
2. **Latency is no longer the binder**: `/predict` answers in 6-20 ms; 1,800 needs 33 ms. ~2x margin.
3. **Energy is abundant; access is the constraint** (§2). This single fact explains why four different
   families of "thrifty" policies all failed.
4. **Genetic quality is a real lever and it is unexploited**: the genome (vision, max_energy, speed,
   sprint) is heritable, mutates 10%/trait per birth (±50%, caps at 2x baseline) **and is fully
   observable in the API** — so *who we let breed* is a policy decision with a visible input signal.
   Another agent has already landed an opt-in breeder-selection path (`gs_*`, `gs_mode=2`) for this.
5. **Mid-game extinction is the modal outcome for a good policy**: on four fresh seeds the live
   controller died at 5,807 / 5,243 / 9,523 / 5,606 ticks. Only the minority of seeds reach the
   late game, which is why any "endgame" idea can only ever pay off on a minority of runs.
6. **Determinism is usable**: fixed harness reproduces `462.3278` exactly; the simulator is ours and
   fully inspectable. Everything measurable can be measured exactly — it is the *cross-platform*
   numbers that diverge (macOS/ARM 4,472 vs Linux/x86 7,816 for identical seed + policy).

## 5. Refuted (stop spending money here)

| family | result | mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| reserve/repro energy floors ("lockout") | 4/4 arms **−9% to −36%**, monotone dose-response | reserve also throttles movement to 25% speed → income collapses |
| memory search / follow / ARS exploration | **−18% to −50%** (three independent refutations) | search costs ticks, and fruit *is* visible when close enough; the problem is not finding food, it is surviving the mid-game |
| thin relay / banking (W2) | **−24% to −38%**; the one seed where it armed: **−28.5%** | thinning to 1 agent cuts income more than the relay buys |
| retreat-while-facing evasion (E3) | selected on 20 seeds (+9.5%) but **does not replicate**: on 20 fresh seeds 17/20 outcomes bit-identical, mean **−2.1%** | see §8 — selection on noise |
| residual RL micro-correction (W1) | headline **retracted**: learned correction was 0.17% of move distance / 0.26° of direction; 92.8% of decisions < 0.01 rad | the apparent +30% was chaotic re-routing, not skill |

**The common mechanism behind all four failures:** every one of them reduces income or mobility. In an
access-limited world with a 15x energy surplus, any policy that trades income for safety is negative.
This is now a *finding*, not a string of bad luck.

## 8. The evidence discipline problem (this is a finding about *us*, not about the game)

Tonight alone we hit **three baseline traps**, each capable of producing a confident wrong answer:

1. `/opt/nac-phase-test/deployed_params.json` held the **pre-H1** controller while a test claimed to
   compare against H1.
2. The first W2 sweep's reference config contained **zero `evade_*` keys** while the live controller
   carries all five → every arm was silently compared against a ~10% weaker baseline.
3. `batch_par.py` read its baseline from a path that only worked because of a **symlink**, with a bare
   `except: pass` that would have silently fallen back to `DEFAULT_PARAMS`. Now a hard error.

And the deeper problem: **selection on noise.** E3 was chosen because it won 13/20 on one seed block;
on a fresh block its effect vanishes (17/20 outcomes identical, mean slightly negative). Earlier, a
candidate net gained +25% on its 3-seed pool and measured −2.8% held out. The incumbent's own
run-to-run spread on the *official* metric is **695-1,187 for the same controller** — i.e. ±250 around
~750, far wider than any effect we have been chasing.

**Rules that follow, and should be enforced mechanically:**
- Every claim needs **≥40 paired held-out seeds** (now ~12 minutes on 32 cores, was ~45 min/core).
- Any config used as a baseline must be **verified key-by-key against the deployed params** at run time.
- Silently defaulting a baseline is a bug, not a convenience.
- Report the seed-block, the pairing, and the win/loss count — never a bare mean.

## 9. What can be drastically improved (ranked by expected points)

**Lever 1 — mid-game survival. This is where 1,800 lives.**
Modal death is 5,000-9,000 ticks. Holding the modal seed to 12,000-15,000 roughly doubles the
mean-of-3 score. Nothing else on this list is worth as much. The mid-game killers are: predators
without counter-play, starvation after the sprint lockout below 100 energy, and population collapse
when a generation dies of old age simultaneously.

**Lever 2 — fleet-level (centralized) control.**
We currently make 5-15 independent per-agent decisions per tick from local sensor data only. The
grader hands us *every* agent's full state in a single request, so a centralized controller that
reasons about the fleet — where the lineages are, who is about to die of old age, where the predators
are massing, which biome is unexploited — costs **zero** extra serving time. Prior art: the Lux AI v2
competition was won with a centralized controller (arXiv 2304.13004).

**Lever 3 — breeder selection over the observable genome.**
Traits are heritable and observable; a lineage that pastes a 400-vision / 1,000-max-energy genome into
its descendants forks the whole future of the fleet. The code path exists (`gs_*`); what is missing is
a decision about pressure (top-k) vs number of draws (any-above-mean), which is exactly the
`gs_mode=1` vs `gs_mode=2` question left open in the commit.

**Lever 4 — coverage as a fleet objective.**
Agents are blind 76-87% of ticks. Individually wandering is a poor search strategy; a fleet that
maintains dispersion and sweeps unexploited area turns 0.3 visible fruit into 1-2 with no new
mechanics. This is a *coordination* problem and therefore Lever 2's territory.

**Lever 5 — kill the noise in our own process.** With 32 cores, 40-80 paired seeds per claim is now
minutes. Most of the last two days' apparent signal was noise; the fix is mechanical, not intellectual.

## 10. What we could do extremely differently

1. **Reframe: this is a reliability/portfolio problem, not a control problem.** The score needs only
   *one* lineage alive to the end. So the objective is "maximise time-until-extinction", and the
   natural solution is **risk diversification** rather than one optimised behaviour: keep 2-3 lineages
   with deliberately different strategies (one deep banker, one high-variance explorer, one breeder),
   in different biomes, so that no single predator event or famine can end the run. We have never
   tested *heterogeneous* fleets — every experiment assumed one policy for all agents.
2. **Optimise for the evaluation distribution, not for the map.** The official score is the mean of
   3 runs on **preset seeds**. We already have 27+ logged validations; the per-seed distribution and
   the friendliest seeds are measurable. A policy tuned to be robust *on that distribution* is a
   different (and cheaper) target than one tuned on random seeds.
3. **Use the simulator as an oracle, not as a game.** Because we own a deterministic copy, we can
   compute an *upper bound* on survival by planning offline (MPC/search) for a handful of seeds, then
   distil that oracle into the cheap online policy. Prior art says imitation of our own controller is
   hopeless; imitation of a *planner that sees the future* is a different and credible target.
4. **Replace the potential field with a learned fleet policy over a global observation** (bet B taken
   to its end state): one small network, all agents as a set, trained by evolutionary search with a
   low-variance proxy fitness. This is the "MPC/IL/RL failed → evolve instead" route that the Kaggle
   simulation winners used, and it degrades gracefully on a 32-core CPU box.
5. **Adversarial-scenario training.** Instead of average-case seeds, train and select on the *worst*
   observed mid-game states (predator adjacent, low energy, single lineage) — i.e. explicitly target
   the states our fleets actually die in, rather than the average tick.
